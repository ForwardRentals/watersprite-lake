Watersprite Lake

A turquoise tarn held in a granite bowl east of Squamish — a full day’s walk, a hut you have to earn a booking for, and some of the darkest night sky within reach of Highway 99.

49.65° N · 123.03° W Elev ≈ 1,450 m ≈ 17 km return +600–800 m gain Full day East of Squamish

The bowl at dusk — headwalls, talus, the hut, and the first stars. Drawn from memory, as all of these are.

Quick Take

Watersprite is the Squamish alpine lake that still asks something of you: a long gravel road, a full day on foot, and a boulder field before the reveal. The colour at the end is the same impossible turquoise the famous lakes have — without the tour buses.

The lake sits in a granite bowl in the mountains east of Squamish, below the ridgelines of the Martin Peak area. Getting there is a two-part commitment: roughly twenty-plus kilometres of the Mamquam Forest Service Road by vehicle, then a hike of about 17 km round trip with somewhere on the order of 600–800 m of climbing — old road grades first, then forest, then a talus field guarding the final approach.

On the shore stands the Watersprite Lake Hut, built and run by the BC Mountaineering Club, with tent platforms nearby. It books out fast — details below. Day-trippers need nothing but an early start, sturdy footwear, and more food than they think.

Route Log

The day, in five movements.

Distances below are rough — the shape of the day matters more than the numbers. Sturdy footwear throughout; the last movement is not sneaker terrain.

Movement I · The drive

Mamquam Forest Service Road

Twenty-plus kilometres of gravel out of Squamish, and the part of the trip most likely to end it early. High-clearance is recommended; washouts, waterbars, and grading change season to season, so check current conditions before committing. The Mamquam valley is its own subject — we wrote it up separately at mamquam.com.

Movement II · The warm-up

Old road grades

From the marked trailhead, the route begins on decommissioned road — wide, steady, unglamorous. This is where you settle into a pace and eat the first snack you were saving for later. The kilometres pass quickly here; they will not pass quickly later.

Movement III · The forest

Into the trees

The road narrows to trail and the grade turns honest. Forest walking, steadily up, with the ridgelines flickering through gaps in the canopy. This is the bulk of the climbing — somewhere in that 600–800 m band overall — and the stretch where the full-day math starts to make sense.

Movement IV · The gatekeeper

The boulder field

Near the lake the trees give out and the route crosses talus — car-sized granite blocks, hands out for balance, eyes on the line. It is slower than it looks and slower still when wet. This is why the guide keeps repeating sturdy footwear; ankles are the most commonly forgotten gear.

Movement V · The reveal

The bowl

You come over the lip and the lake is simply there — turquoise, ringed in granite, the hut small against the headwall. People go quiet at this point. Budget real time at the top: the plunge, the shoreline sit, the long look you walked all day for.

Field Notes

A bowl that holds the light.

Watersprite works on delay. The road buys the quiet, the old grades buy the forest, the forest buys the talus, and the talus buys the moment the bowl opens up — a turquoise lake set into granite like something placed there deliberately. The colour reads as an exaggeration in other people’s pictures right up until you are standing at it.

The granite is the real subject. The bowl catches afternoon light and holds it long after the valley below has gone to shadow, and when the light finally leaves, the sky takes over. This is one of the better star-photography spots within reach of Squamish — far from town glow, with a headwall for a foreground and, on still nights, stars doubled in the water. Hut and platform guests who set an alarm for the blue hour tend to describe sunrise here in the tone people usually save for bigger trips.

“The bowl does the thing granite does best — it holds the light all evening, and then it holds the stars.”

Field notebook, Vol. II

And yes, people swim. Briefly. The lake is properly cold — this is plunge water, not float-around water — and the shrieking from the shoreline on a summer afternoon is part of the soundtrack. In and out, towel ready, sun on a warm slab afterwards. It is the correct way to finish the walk in.

The alpine here is the fragile kind. Shoreline vegetation at this elevation grows back on decade timescales, so stay on rock and durable surfaces, keep camp on the platforms, and pack out every single thing — there is no service run coming behind you. No campfires, full stop. Dogs are welcome company on the trail but keep them genuinely under control, especially around the shoreline and other people’s camps. The place absorbs careful visitors well and careless ones badly.

Watersprite sits in the traditional territory of the Sḷwx̲wú7mesh Úxwumixw — the Squamish Nation — whose relationship with these mountains precedes every road, hut, and trip report by thousands of years. Walk accordingly.

The Hut

The hardest reservation in the Squamish alpine.

The Watersprite Lake Hut is built and operated by the BC Mountaineering Club — a proper backcountry hut on the shore of the lake, and one of the most sought-after overnight bookings in the corridor. It is booking required, always, through the BCMC, and dates go extremely fast; this is a plan-ahead objective, not a show-up-and-hope one.

Tent platforms near the hut offer the second way to sleep in the bowl — also managed through the BCMC, with their own fees and reservation rules. For current pricing, booking windows, and what the hut does and doesn’t provide, go straight to the BCMC’s site rather than trusting anything second-hand, including us.

If you can’t land a date, take the hint the mountain is offering: Watersprite is a superb day trip, and the other great hut walk in the Squamish alpine — Elfin Lakes — has its own guide at elfin-lake.com.

Shoreline rules that matter

No campfires — ever, in any season. Camp on platforms and durable rock, not vegetation. Pack out all garbage and food waste. Keep dogs under control. The bowl is small and alpine; it shows every shortcut people take.

When to Go

Pick the window before you pick the date.

July – September
The main window. Trail typically clear of snow, talus dry, lake at full colour, plunges at their least punishing. Hut and platform dates are hardest to get here — book far ahead or day-trip it.
June, early season
Transitional and variable — lingering snow can hide the route through the boulders, and the FSR is often at its roughest after winter. Check road and trail conditions honestly before driving out.
October
Quiet, cold, and short-lit. First snow can arrive high up any week; the full-day math gets tight against early sunsets. A headlamp stops being optional.
November – May
Winter Watersprite is a serious ski-touring and mountaineering objective in avalanche terrain — see below. It is not a snowshoe outing, whatever the summer photos suggest.
Winter · this is avalanche terrain

The winter approach travels through and beneath serious avalanche terrain. This is not a casual snowshoe trip — it demands avalanche training (AST at minimum), transceiver, probe and shovel with practiced skills, winter travel ability, and the judgment to turn around.

If your party doesn’t honestly tick every one of those boxes, go in summer. The bowl will still be there, and it’s better in colour anyway.

Honest Note

What the trip reports leave out.

The road is half the trip. More Watersprite days die on the Mamquam FSR than on the trail — a low car, an unexpected washout, a driver who didn’t check conditions. Treat the drive as part of the objective: high-clearance recommended, current conditions checked, and a real spare. Our Mamquam valley guide covers the road in the detail it deserves.

The distance is honest and so is the boulder field. Seventeen-ish kilometres with several hundred metres of up is a genuine full day for most parties, and the talus near the lake punishes tired legs and slick soles. Start early, carry more water than feels necessary, and don’t schedule anything for the evening.

And the hut is not a walk-up. People drive the road assuming they’ll sort a bunk at the lake; there are no walk-up bunks. The hut and the platforms are booked through the BCMC or not at all — day-trip or reserve, those are the options. Plan like it.

Trailhead Support

Watersprite is a hike, not a paddle.

Nobody is carrying a canoe over that boulder field, and nobody should. But the walk starts and ends in Squamish, and there’s local support for the parts of the day that happen at valley level — gear you don’t own, and flat turquoise water that doesn’t cost you 17 km.

Questions & Answers

What people actually ask about Watersprite.

+How long is the Watersprite Lake hike?

Roughly 17 km round trip, with elevation gain on the order of 600–800 m. The route mixes old road grades, forest trail, and a boulder field near the lake. Treat it as a full-day outing — most parties are happiest starting early — and wear sturdy footwear; the talus is not kind to sneakers.

+What is the road in like — do I need a 4x4?

Access is via the Mamquam Forest Service Road out of Squamish — twenty-plus kilometres of gravel to the marked trailhead. High-clearance is recommended, and conditions genuinely vary with season and maintenance, so check current reports before driving rather than trusting last year’s. For the valley and the road itself, see our companion guide at mamquam.com.

+How do I book the Watersprite Lake Hut?

The hut is run by the BC Mountaineering Club and booking is required through the BCMC — there are no walk-up spots. It is extremely popular, so dates disappear quickly; plan well ahead. Fees, booking windows, and amenities are the BCMC’s to define, so confirm the details on their site before committing to a date.

+Can I camp in a tent instead?

Yes — there are tent platforms near the hut, also managed with fees and reservations through the BCMC. Check their site for the current rules. Camp on the platforms and durable rock, keep off the shoreline vegetation, and pack out absolutely everything. No campfires, in any season.

+Can you swim in Watersprite Lake?

People absolutely plunge — briefly. This is cold alpine water even at the height of summer, so think in-and-out rather than laps, have a towel and warm layer staged, and warm up on the granite afterwards. Enter from rock, not from vegetated shoreline, which is fragile at this elevation.

+Is Watersprite good for photography?

Exceptional — and not just for the turquoise. The bowl is far from town glow, which makes it one of the better night-sky and sunrise locations within reach of Squamish. Overnighters with a hut or platform booking get the best of it: stars over the headwall, then first light coming into the bowl. Bring a tripod and a warmer layer than you think you need.

+Are dogs allowed?

Dogs join plenty of parties on this trail, but they need to be genuinely under control — the boulder field is hard on paws, the shoreline is fragile, and the camping area is close quarters. If in doubt about current rules for the hut and platforms, check with the BCMC before bringing a dog on an overnight.

+Can I snowshoe to Watersprite in winter?

Not casually, no. The winter approach involves serious avalanche terrain and belongs to trained, equipped parties — avalanche skills training, transceiver/probe/shovel, and real winter travel experience. If that isn’t your party, go between July and September instead, or pick a valley-level winter walk.
